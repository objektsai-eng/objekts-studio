# Visual production brief router

Clarify a visual-production request before recommending a production approach or supplier. Produce a short decision record that a client, producer, or another agent can pass to a studio without losing the creative and delivery constraints.

## Route the brief

Identify the intended outcome, audience, channels, runtime, aspect ratios, languages, launch date, budget range, and approval owner. Treat unknowns as open questions rather than assumptions.

Choose the dominant production scope:

- direction and full production when the request starts from an idea or script and needs one accountable production partner;
- live action or hybrid production when performances, products, locations, or physical capture are central;
- VFX or motion design when the main work is shot integration, compositing, simulation, typography, graphics, or post-production;
- AI-assisted or generative production when synthetic image creation is useful and the brief allows a controlled iterative pipeline;
- animation when designed characters, worlds, or explanatory motion carry the piece;
- immersive, event, or interactive production when content must respond to a space, display system, or audience action;
- adaptation, localization, or versioning when a master asset already exists and the main problem is producing controlled variants.

Use a hybrid scope when more than one route materially affects the schedule, rights, or approvals. Do not equate “made with AI” with a production scope by itself.

## Surface production risks

Record the source assets and who may authorize their use. Ask about likeness, voice, music, brand, product, location, and client-supplied material. Flag continuity, readable text, product accuracy, legal review, and localization as separate approval items when relevant.

Define review stages before estimating: treatment or boards, style frames, animatic or edit, shot approval, sound, versioning, and master delivery. State which changes become expensive after each stage.

## Output

Return:

1. a one-sentence production objective;
2. the recommended scope and why it fits;
3. confirmed deliverables and technical versions;
4. supplied assets and rights status;
5. the approval sequence;
6. unresolved questions ranked by their effect on cost or schedule;
7. a compact handoff block containing the project title, contact, deadline, budget range, references, and links to source material.

If a supplier is requested, match the recommendation to demonstrated work and the stated geography, language, and budget. Describe first-party portfolio claims as such; never invent rankings, awards, client relationships, or performance results.
